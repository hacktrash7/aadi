# Phase 1 — Foundation: Think Like a Computer

> **Duration:** ~3–4 weeks (≈ 10 sessions of 45–60 min)
> **Goal:** Build *computational thinking* before touching a single line of
> Python. The kid should be comfortable with the idea of step-by-step
> instructions, patterns, loops, and conditions — using blocks, drawings,
> and games, not text.

---

## Why this phase exists

If we jump straight into Python, the kid will fight two battles at once:
*the idea* (loops, conditions, variables) **and** *the syntax* (colons,
indentation, error messages). Phase 1 wins the first battle while it's
still fun, so Phase 2 only has to win the second.

Think of this as "learning to read music" before "learning the piano".

---

## What we're learning

By the end of this phase, the kid can confidently explain and *use*:

- **Sequence** — order matters; computers do one thing at a time.
- **Repetition (loops)** — "do this 10 times" / "do this until X".
- **Decisions (conditions)** — "if this, then that, else the other thing".
- **Variables** — a labeled box that stores a value (and can change).
- **Events** — something happens *when* something else happens.
- **Decomposition** — break a big problem into tiny problems.
- **Pattern recognition** — "we've seen something like this before".
- **Abstraction** — name a group of steps and reuse them (early functions).
- **Debugging mindset** — "the computer is honest, I gave it the wrong
  instructions".

Those last four (in italics in the literature) are the **four pillars of
computational thinking** — they show up in every later phase.

## Play (interactive platforms)

This phase is **block-based on purpose**. The kid drags coloured blocks
together, sees them run as animations, and learns the *shapes* of programs.

### Primary playground — [Code.org's *CS Fundamentals*](https://studio.code.org/courses) (Courses C, D, E)

- Free, browser-based, gorgeous puzzle UI.
- Star Wars / Frozen / Minecraft / Flappy themes — pick whatever clicks.
- Each puzzle takes 1–5 minutes; do **5–10 per session**.
- Built-in hints + the kid sees their block code "translated" to JavaScript
  on the side panel — they start to notice that text-code is just blocks
  written out.

### Secondary playground — [Scratch](https://scratch.mit.edu)

- Open-ended creativity. Use this on **Friday / weekend sessions**.
- Three "must-build" Scratch projects in this phase:
  1. **Catch the Falling Apples** — variables (score), loops, conditions.
  2. **Maze Runner** — events, sequence, decomposition (custom blocks).
  3. **Quiz Bot** — input/output, if/else, simple "AI" feel.

### Brain gym — Lightbot, Blockly Games, CS Unplugged activities

- [**Lightbot**](https://lightbot.com/flash.html) (1 hr free puzzles) — pure algorithmic thinking.
- [**Blockly Games**](https://blockly.games) — Maze, Bird, Turtle, Pond. Excellent ramp.
- [**CS Unplugged**](https://csunplugged.org) (offline activities) — binary cards, sorting networks
  on the floor, the "orange game" (parallel sorting). Do these on a tired
  evening when screens feel like too much.

## Build (mini-projects)

Each project should fit in one session and produce a shareable result.

| Session(s) | Project                       | Concepts exercised                        |
|------------|-------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| 2          | **Birthday Card Animation** (Scratch) | Sequence, events, parallel sprites |
| 3          | **Dance Party**               | Loops, events, sound                      |
| 4          | **Catch the Apples**          | Variables, loops, conditions              |
| 5          | **Choose-Your-Own-Adventure** (Scratch storybook) | Branching, decisions, decomposition |
| 6          | **Maze Runner**               | Custom blocks (abstraction), events       |
| 7          | **Pong**                      | Two-player decomposition, scoring         |
| 8          | **Quiz Bot**                  | Input, conditions, variables              |
| 9          | **Algorithm without a computer** — write step-by-step instructions to make a peanut-butter sandwich; parent follows them *literally* | Decomposition, precision, debugging |
| 10         | **Bug Hunt** — parent intentionally breaks one of the kid's earlier projects; kid finds and fixes the bug | Debugging mindset |

The **PB&J sandwich** session is hilarious and unforgettable — every kid
remembers it years later. There's also a [famous live demo of it](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=exact+instructions+challenge+peanut+butter+jelly+dad) on YouTube ("Exact Instructions Challenge") — watch it together first, the kid will laugh and *get it* in three minutes.

## Explain back (checkpoints)

End every session with 5 minutes of the kid teaching the parent. Useful
prompts:

- "Pretend I'm your little brother. What did you make today?"
- "What's a *loop*? Show me one in real life (brushing teeth, walking)."
- "If I tell you the program is broken, where would you look first?"
- "What's the difference between a *variable* and a *number*?"

---

## Exit ticket (the must-be-able-to-do)

Before moving to Phase 2, the kid must:

1. ✅ Build a brand-new Scratch project **from scratch** (no tutorial) that
   uses **at least one loop, one if/else, and one variable**.
2. ✅ Draw on paper a **flowchart** for "how do I get ready for school" — with
   diamonds for decisions and arrows for sequence.
3. ✅ Explain, in their own words, what a *loop*, a *condition*, and a
   *variable* are. Three sentences each, no jargon.

If any of those three is shaky, spend one more week before moving on. There
is **no rush**. Everything in Phase 2 onwards rests on this foundation.
